The Backtest That Lied

QuantBot trades BTC perpetual futures on a schedule, unattended, on a free-tier ARM box that costs nothing to run. For months it was validated against a backtest reporting +90.7% a year. The real figure is +13.7%. This is the story of two bugs that cancelled each other out inside the one invariant I thought to check — and what it took to trust a number I had generated myself.

01The Brief: A Program That Can Lose Money

Discretionary trading fails on two axes, and both of them are mechanical. Nobody watches a 15-minute chart around the clock. And everybody overrides their own rules at precisely the moment the rules are earning their keep — after a losing streak, when the next signal looks like the one that finally works.

Both failure modes are removable by writing them out of the loop. The signal becomes a function. The position size becomes a formula. The cooling-off period becomes a circuit breaker that enforces the timeout a human would have talked themselves out of.

So: QuantBot. BTC/USDT perpetual futures, 15-minute candles, one decision per closed candle, running unattended on a free-tier ARM VM and reporting everything it does to Telegram and a public dashboard.

The trading logic is about 1,500 lines of Python. Everything around it — Terraform-provisioned infrastructure, a multi-stage Docker build, a deployment pipeline that refuses to restart the bot mid-trade, public HTTPS through zero open ports, a Telegram control plane — is the part I would actually point at in an interview. A trading bot is a small distributed system that happens to have opinions about RSI, and the distributed-system half is where the engineering lives.

This post is mostly about neither. It is about the day I found out the numbers were wrong.

"An invariant that holds is not proof the model is right. It can just as easily be two errors cancelling."

02Three Processes, One Volume, No Database

Three Python processes share one Docker named volume, and files are the only IPC. There is no database, no queue, and no message bus.

ProcessRole
bot.py The trading engine, and the only writer of trade state. Wakes six seconds after each 15-minute boundary, discards the still-forming candle, and makes exactly one decision.
notifier.py The only process that talks to Telegram. One 60-second loop running five independent components: trade alerts, a heartbeat monitor, an RSI radar across six spot pairs, a daily summary, and a command handler.
dashboard.py A Plotly Dash UI that reads every state file and writes nothing. There is no write action anywhere in the interface.

Why files and not a database. One host, three processes, exactly one writer per file, and state you can cat over SSH at three in the morning. Postgres or Redis would add an operational failure mode and buy nothing at this scale. The costs of that choice are real — non-atomic writes and no file locking — and they are on the limitations list rather than swept under it.

The coupling between the three is deliberately unsophisticated:

  • A closed trade is the notifier noticing that trade_log.csv gained a row.
  • An opened position is position.entry_time changing inside bot_state.json.
  • A crashed or wedged bot is the wall-clock field last_updated_at going quiet for thirty minutes.

That third one deserves its own paragraph, because it is a fossil. The heartbeat originally compared now against last_candle_ts — the candle's own timestamp. A candle stamped 16:00 and processed at 16:30 looks instantly half an hour stale, so the monitor fired a crash alert on every single candle, forever. The fix was to add a separate wall-clock field that means "the bot was alive at this moment" rather than "this is the bar we last looked at."

last_updated_at exists for exactly that reason. It looks redundant next to last_candle_ts until you understand what it is measuring, which is roughly the definition of a comment worth writing.

03The Signal, and Why It's Locked

An entry requires all three gates to fire on the same closed 15-minute candle. The selectivity is the entire edge: 

signalGate 1 — RSI(14) divergence          armed for DIV_MEMORY = 3 candles
    Bullish:  price makes a lower low   +  RSI makes a higher low
    Bearish:  price makes a higher high +  RSI makes a lower high
    Detected over a rolling min/max window — deliberately NOT
    pivot-based swing detection (see below)

Gate 2 — MACD(12/26/9) cross         timing confirmation

Gate 3 — Volume spike                participation confirmation
    volume > 2.0 × its 20-bar SMA
    The binding constraint — most candles die here

        ↓ all three aligned ↓

ENTRY   BTC/USDT perp · isolated margin · 5× leverage
EXIT    opposite three-gate signal, or liquidation. Nothing else.

That produces roughly 18 trades a year, held about 18 days each. It is a swing strategy that happens to trigger on 15-minute candles, and calling it anything else would set the wrong expectations about what the numbers later in this post mean.

Gate 1 carries a scar. Swing detection originally marked a high or low at bar i using bars i+1 through i+5 — data that does not exist at bar i in real time. The backtest that produced was worth $2.4 billion, which is the useful kind of wrong: absurd enough to be caught immediately. It is why the live detector uses a rolling min/max window rather than confirmed pivots. Subtler versions of the same mistake are the subject of the next section.

The order of operations is load-bearing

QuantBot.process() runs once per closed candle, in this order:

1. DCA check                — monthly contribution on DCA_DAY
2. Snapshot the corpus      ← the non-obvious one
3. Update armed counters    — fresh divergence resets, else decay by 1
4. Funding                  — real 8h settlements, 00:00 / 08:00 / 16:00 UTC
5. Exits                    — liquidation (intrabar), then opposite signal
6. Entries                  — only if flat and not paused

An exit and a fresh entry can both happen on the same candle. That is why step 2 exists: position size must be computed from the corpus as of the candle open, so that an exit's profit ratchet cannot inflate the entry that follows it on the same bar. Reading the live corpus at entry time instead — which is what the code did originally, and which looks completely reasonable — drifts the two engines apart to a profit factor of 1.59 against 1.63 over 6.9 years.

One line's worth of ordering, two and a half percent of profit factor. The strategy parameters live in .env rather than in code, and they are frozen: changing any of them invalidates every number this post cites.

04The Backtest That Lied

For months, the repository headlined this: $100 → $9,347. +90.7% a year. Profit factor 1.60. Measured over 6.9 years spanning a COVID crash, two bull cycles and the FTX collapse.

Printed directly underneath it, in the same table, was a maximum drawdown of 184.2%.

That is the tell, and I walked past it for months. Drawdown is (peak − equity) / peak. It cannot exceed 100% unless equity went negative. The simulated account had gone bankrupt, and the backtest had carried on trading it — because nothing in the engine checked for ruin, and nothing compared the margin a trade required against the balance actually available to fund it.

Underneath that were two lines of code that had been wrong since the beginning:

close_position — P&L
raw_pnl = (exit − entry) × qty × LEVERAGE
size_position — quantity
qty = dollar_risk / (stop_distance × LEVERAGE)

Real futures P&L never multiplies by leverage. Profit and loss is Δprice × qty. Leverage decides how much margin the exchange locks up and where the liquidation price sits, and it does nothing else. Multiplying by it is not an approximation; it is a category error.

So why did roughly twenty backtests, several months, and an entire written specification fail to notice? Because the two errors cancel exactly for a loss at the stop. Dividing the size by 20 and then multiplying the P&L by 20 gives you back the number you started with — for that one case. And that one case was the only invariant anyone ever checked: a stopped-out trade should lose 10% of the corpus. It did. Perfectly. Every time.

Everything that was not that specific case was wrong by a factor of twenty.

It was caught by a screenshot

Not by a test — there aren't any. By looking at the dashboard and seeing a trade row reporting Invested $7.02 alongside P&L −$15.15.

Under isolated margin, losing more than you posted is not a bad outcome, it is an impossible one. So one of those two numbers was a lie, and the interesting question was which. The way in was the fee column — the only field in the row with no leverage term anywhere in its derivation. Solving the row backwards from the fee pinned LEVERAGE = 20.0 sitting in both the P&L formula and the "invested" formula, and showed the real margin on that trade had been $139.63.

Three consequences, in ascending order of how bad they were
  • Stored quantity, stored margin, and the dashboard's "Invested $" were all off by 20×. Cosmetic, in the sense that no money moved.
  • Live mode would have sent Binance an order 20× too small while booking 20× the P&L into its own ledger. The bot's balance and the exchange's would have diverged on the very first trade, and the bot would have believed itself.
  • Fees are a fraction of notional, and notional was 1/20th of the truth. Every backtest in the project's history had undercounted trading costs by 20×.

That last one is the expensive one, because the error scales with trade count. A strategy that trades twice a month barely notices. A strategy that trades six hundred times gets a subsidy. Which means the bug had spent months quietly putting its thumb on the scale in favour of every high-frequency idea I had ever tested against it.

05What the Correction Cost

With a ruin check and a margin-affordability cap added, and leverage removed from the P&L, the same signal over the same 6.9 years produces this:

Configuration Final CAGR PF Win rate Verdict
20× · ATR stop $789 −5.7% 0.92 12.9% rejected
5× · ATR stop $2,847 +16.8% 1.16 27.1% rejected — see below
5× · no stop $2,569 +13.7% 1.63 54.0% ✓ selected

The headline went from +90.7% a year to +13.7% a year, and from $9,347 to $2,569. Note that the middle row still looks like the winner on terminal equity, and it was rejected anyway — which is the more interesting half of the story.

A robustness suite ran four tests on each candidate: an out-of-sample split, a 10,000-sample bootstrap on profit factor, a profit-concentration check, and per-year consistency. The no-stop model clears all four. The stop model fails three, and fails the concentration test in the most damning way available: its entire 6.9-year net profit is a single trade. One position contributed +$1,674 against a total net of +$1,540. Remove that one trade and the strategy is net negative across seven years.

A high terminal equity produced by one lucky position is not an edge. It is a lottery ticket with a backtest wrapped around it.

The research history was collateral damage

Re-running the archive on the corrected engine turned several settled conclusions inside out:

  • A 15-minute mean-reversion strategy scored $8,205 at PF 1.21. Corrected, it returns $870 at PF 0.90 — a losing system. Its 602 trades, each paying 1/20th of its real fees, were the entire edge.
  • The parameter sensitivity sweep survived intact. Perturbing every signal parameter one step in each direction leaves 18 of 18 neighbouring configurations profitable. That is a plateau, not a fitted spike, and it is the single result that most makes me believe the remaining edge is real.
  • The "BTC only" verdict survived too. Per-symbol profit factor: BTC 1.63, XRP 1.19, SOL 1.08, ETH 1.05, BNB 0.55. A five-symbol portfolio scores PF 1.03 at 82.6% drawdown — the alts are correlated enough that trading all five is placing the same bet five times while calling it diversification.

"A fee bug scales with trade count, which means it had been quietly subsidising every high-frequency idea I ever tested."

06Sizing When There Is No Stop-Loss

The configuration that survived all four robustness tests removes the stop-loss entirely.

python# size_position_nostop() — margin-first
margin   = corpus * RISK_PER_TRADE   # 10% of corpus — IS the maximum loss
notional = margin * LEVERAGE
qty      = notional / price

With no stop there is no stop distance to size against, so the margin becomes the risk decision directly. Under isolated margin you cannot lose more than the margin posted, which means the loss ceiling is enforced by the exchange rather than by an order that might fail to place.

That clause is the whole argument, and it is worth being explicit about why removing a safety feature made the system safer.

The stop model's worst case is this: the STOP_MARKET order fails to place, three retries all fail, the code logs CRITICAL and falls back to checking the stop itself once per candle — and then the process dies. You are now holding a leveraged position with no protective order and nothing running to close it. The loss is bounded by nothing in particular.

The no-stop model's worst case is that the position is liquidated at roughly 19.6% away from entry — 1/leverage minus the maintenance margin rate — and you lose the 10% of corpus you posted. That ceiling holds whether or not the bot ever runs again, because it is the exchange's rule and not the program's intention.

It is also about eight times cheaper to operate. Under the stop model, margin / corpus = RISK / (stop_pct × LEVERAGE), which reaches roughly 1.0 for a 2% stop at 5× — meaning every trade posted the entire account as margin. The no-stop model posts a flat 10%. Across the 6.9-year backtest, total fees fall from $581 to $64.

The one setting that silently breaks all of this

Isolated margin is the only thing capping a losing trade in this model. If auto-add-margin is enabled on the exchange, Binance tops the position up from the wallet balance and the cap quietly stops being a cap. The bot logs a warning at startup and cannot enforce it — it is a checkbox in someone else's UI. It is the single most important item on the go-live checklist, and it is the kind of dependency worth writing down in three places.

One asymmetry in the exit logic is deliberate. Every exit in this bot triggers on the candle close — except liquidation, which is checked intrabar against the candle's high and low. The exchange does not wait for the close, so checking the close would hide most liquidations behind wicks and produce a backtest that quietly survives events the real account would not have.

07Proving the Live Code Matches the Backtest

The gate for putting real money in is: twenty or more paper trades, with win rate and profit factor within ±20% of the backtested 54.0% and 1.63.

That gate is worth precisely nothing unless the live engine and the backtest engine actually agree. Two independently written implementations of "the same" strategy is exactly the setup that produced the last disaster.

So the strategy is validated by replay: 6.9 years of real candles pushed through bot.py's own process() function — the live code path, not a copy of it — and diffed against the backtest trade by trade.

Closing that gap found three more real bugs, two of which were affecting live behaviour:

  • A ratchet guard that ran zero times. The condition if pnl > 0 or not (pnl <= 0 and cb_pause_until) meant that a loss closing during an older circuit-breaker pause never reached the position-sizing module at all. Calling it unconditionally, exactly once per trade, moved profit factor from 1.59 to 1.63.
  • Corpus read at entry time rather than snapshotted at the candle open — the same-candle ordering problem from section 03. Found here, not by reading the code.
  • A start-year mismatch between .env and the backtest harness. Unglamorous, and worth about a third of the initial gap.

"Reproducing the backtest from the live code path is the only test that makes a paper-versus-benchmark gate mean anything."

The honest footnote: the deprecated stop model is not engine-exact and never was. The bot prices a stop exit at the stop; the backtest prices it at the candle close, which by definition is past it. Over identical candles the bot finishes at $6,275 where its own backtest says $2,847. That gap is not a rounding difference, it is a systematic optimism — and it is a second, independent reason that model must not be run with real money.

The other honest footnote: there is no unit test suite. CI runs flake8 --select=E9,F63,F7,F82 and py_compile — syntax errors and undefined names, nothing semantic. The replay harness is the test suite, and it is a good one for exactly one property. size_position, the circuit breaker, and the corpus ratchet are pure functions with obvious invariants and no excuse; they are the first three things I would test. Saying so is better than implying a coverage that isn't there.

08Infrastructure That Won't Close Your Position

The deployment pipeline has one design goal that overrides the others: a code push must never be able to close a live position. 

Push to main
  │
  ▼
1. Lint gate          flake8 E9,F63,F7,F82 + py_compile × 4 modules
  │                    deploy does not run if this fails
  ▼
2. Detect changes     git diff HEAD~1 HEAD → BOT / NOTIFIER / DASHBOARD / INFRA
  │
  ▼
3. 🔒 Safety gate     read bot_state.json over SSH — position open?
  │                      open → the bot container is NOT restarted,
  │                      even during a full infra rebuild.
  │                      Everything else rebuilds around it.
  ▼
4. Copy               scp an explicit allow-list — never "."
  │                      .env on the server is NEVER overwritten
  ▼
5. Selective restart  only the services that actually changed
  │
  ▼
6. Health check       docker inspect each container, dump logs on failure

Step 3 is the one I would defend in a review. A deploy is a routine, low-drama event right up until the moment it restarts a process that is holding a leveraged position, at which point it becomes a financial event.

The nginx bug that cost real hours

nginx.conf, the htpasswd file and the error pages are bind-mounted from disk rather than baked into the image. And docker compose up only recreates a container when the service definition changes — not when a bind-mounted file does.

So config edits were being copied to the server correctly, landing on disk correctly, and then never read by the running process. Every change appeared to deploy successfully and did nothing at all. CI now force-recreates nginx on any infrastructure change, which is a one-line fix for a failure mode that gives you no error message whatsoever.

Free tier, and the fight to get on it

Everything is Terraform against Oracle Cloud's always-free tier: VCN, gateway, route table, security list, subnet, and a VM.Standard.A1.Flex ARM instance. Six resources, one apply, $0 a month.

The shape is deliberately 1 OCPU and 6 GB rather than the full 4 and 24 the free tier allows — the always-free ARM pool is capacity-constrained, and a smaller shape schedules far more reliably. Getting an instance at all took a retry script cycling availability domains on a five-minute interval, a support ticket escalated to Sev-2, and eventually an upgrade to pay-as-you-go, which grants access to the paid capacity pool while staying inside always-free resource limits and therefore still billing nothing.

One line in that Terraform is worth more attention than it looks: boot_volume_vpus_per_gb = 10. Ten is "Balanced", which is always-free. Twenty is faster, looks like a harmless performance tweak, and silently starts charging you.

Public HTTPS with nothing listening

The dashboard is bound to 127.0.0.1 inside the host and reachable only through nginx. nginx is reachable only through a Cloudflare Tunnel, which is an outbound connection to Cloudflare's edge.

The result: real HTTPS on a real domain with zero inbound ports open, no certificate renewal cron, and the origin IP absent from DNS. The conventional alternative — certbot plus an open 443 — needs an inbound port, a 90-day renewal that will eventually fail on a holiday, and publishes your server's address to anyone who asks.

The control plane is a chat window

notifier.py pushes trade opens and closes, circuit-breaker trips, crash alerts, DCA contributions, RSI extremes, and a daily summary at 00:00 UTC. It also accepts commands — /status, /balance, /pos, /pause, /resume — using long-polling rather than a webhook, which means the control plane needs no inbound port either.

Every incoming command has its chat_id verified against the configured one; anything else is logged and dropped. The chat ID is the entire authorization model, which is honest about the scale this thing runs at: one operator, one account, one conversation.

/pause is the design detail I like most. It touches a flag file, and bot.py checks for that file before opening a position — but never before closing one. An operator kill-switch must not be able to trap a live trade. Pausing stops the bot taking new risk; it does not strand the risk already on the table.

09The Gap Between the Repo and the Machine

This post would be dishonest without the following section, which is also the most useful one in it.

CI deliberately never overwrites the server's .env. That is how exchange keys and strategy parameters stay out of the repository, and it is the right call. The consequence is that a strategy change committed to git is invisible to production, and nothing anywhere warns you about it.

So the repository says EXIT_MODEL=nostop at 5×. The deployed .env has no EXIT_MODEL key at all — which means it falls back to the code default of stop, at LEVERAGE=20, on the pre-August ATR multipliers. The dashboard you can open right now is running the configuration that was rejected.

Note what that dashboard is honestly telling on itself about. A 14.3% win rate sits beside its own benchmark of 54.0%, so the comparison is right there. But the top row also reports +608.8% total return and an annualised +1,379% from seven trades — a number that is arithmetically correct and epistemically worthless. The dashboard suppresses annualisation below 30 days or 5 trades; 161 days and 7 trades clears both gates and produces nonsense anyway. Thresholds are not judgement.

And that is the part I find genuinely valuable. The robustness suite rejected the stop model because its entire net profit was one trade. Seven live paper trades later, the deployed bot has independently reproduced exactly that shape: one winner carrying six losers, and a monthly P&L chart that is a single green bar in a row of red ones.

A backtest finding and a live result agreeing on a failure mode is close to the best validation available — even when the thing being validated is "do not run this."

The fix is four lines in a file on one VM plus a state reset, and it is the top item on the roadmap. But "the repository and the machine disagree, and no tooling anywhere notices" is a real class of production problem that outlives this project. The durable fix is not to remember harder; it is to have the bot log its full effective configuration at startup, so the log itself is auditable and the drift shows up the first time anyone looks.

The rest of the honest list

  • BlockerThe CI safety gate fails open. The open-position probe runs as a bare docker exec with errors swallowed into || echo "none". If the deploy user ever lost docker-group membership, the probe would error, report "no position", and restart the bot mid-trade. A safety gate must fail closed.
  • BlockerState writes are not atomic. save_state writes straight over bot_state.json with no temp file and no backup. A crash mid-write corrupts it. Write to .tmp and os.replace — five lines.
  • DeployThe containerised bot cannot trade live as configured. The Dockerfile's CMD has no --live flag and compose overrides no command, so the container runs in paper mode whatever .env says. That is fail-safe in the right direction, and it is also the least obvious step in the entire go-live checklist.
  • SecurityThe dashboard is public and unauthenticated. auth_basic is fully wired into the nginx config and deliberately commented out for the building-in-public phase. It renders balances and positions but never keys — a disclosure choice, not a leak. It gets switched back on before real capital.
  • SecurityEvery container gets the same .env, so exchange keys sit in the environment of two processes that never trade — one of which serves internet traffic. Split into .env.bot and .env.shared.
  • QualityBenchmarks are duplicated in the bot and the dashboard, and leverage is read independently by a third place. Three values to keep in sync by hand is two too many.
Stating the obvious, because it is a trading bot

None of this is financial advice. Backtested performance is a measurement of how a set of rules would have behaved on data that has already happened — it is not a prediction, and this post is substantially about how wrong such a measurement can be while looking entirely healthy. The project runs in paper mode; no real capital is deployed.

Why the Smaller Number Is the Better One

The version of this post I could have written a month earlier had far better numbers in it. $9,347. +90.7% a year. A profit factor of 1.60 across two bull markets and a bear. Every one of those was false, and not one of them was false because anyone was trying to fool anyone. They were false because two bugs cancelled in the exact place I had thought to look.

What replaced them: +13.7% a year, eighteen trades a year, a 25.5% maximum drawdown, and a live engine that reproduces its own backtest to the cent across 126 trades. That is a substantially less impressive set of numbers and the first set I have any reason to believe.

The transferable lesson is not about trading. It is that a derived metric with no independent check on it is a rumour with a decimal point. The check that eventually worked was not a unit test and could not have been — it was reproducing the same result through a second, independently written code path and refusing to accept anything less than agreement to the cent. When they disagreed, the disagreement handed over three more bugs.

And a cheaper lesson, which cost less and has been more immediately useful since: when a number looks absurd, solve the arithmetic backwards from whichever field has the fewest terms in it. A $7 position cannot lose $15. The fee column was the only one with no leverage anywhere in it, and the fee column is what gave the bug up.
